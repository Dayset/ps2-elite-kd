01 Damage model

How many bullets a kill takes is arithmetic, not feel. Learn the tiers and you can read any weapon's card at a glance.

  • Health pools: standard infantry 1000 HP (500 health + 500 shields). Infiltrator 900 (500 + 400). MAX 2000 HP, no shields.
  • Falloff is linear: full damage out to the max-damage range, then a straight line down to minimum damage, then flat. Example from the articles: TRAC-5, 143 @ 10 m → 112 @ 60 m.
  • Bullets to kill: BTK = ceil(target HP ÷ bullet damage). One point of damage can change the count — the TRAC-5 needs 7 bullets at 143 (7 × 143 = 1001) but 8 at 142 (7 × 142 = 994). Most automatics drop two damage tiers over their range; SMGs typically drop four.
  • Rounding: damage reduced by resistances or armour rounds up; damage reduced by range degradation rounds down.
  • Headshots ×2 for most weapons, and they ignore Nanoweave. Exceptions: bolt-action snipers 2.1×, shotguns / T7 Mini-Chaingun / Hunter crossbow 1.5×, NS-61 Emissary 1.8×, NSX Tomoe 2.5×, NSX Daimyo 5×. At ×2, 143- and 125-damage bullets need 4 headshots; 200- and 167-damage bullets need 3.
  • Legs ×0.9 for all known small arms and machine guns. Arms and body take normal damage. The weapon model is ignored by bullets — only the body part hit matters.
  • Hit markers: plain hit = cross, headshot = double cross, any hit over 250 damage adds a circle.
  • DPS and TTK: DPS = damage × RoF ÷ 60; TTK = (BTK − 1) ÷ (RoF ÷ 60). TRAC-5: DPS 1787, TTK 0.48 s. The articles give average TTK as ~0.5 s.

Alpha vs rate of fire

High bullet damage wins the first shot: a 200-damage gun has done 200 with its first bullet while a 125-damage gun has done 125, and rate of fire only matters from the second shot on. High damage also finishes wounded targets and punishes less when you can only land single shots on a partly exposed enemy. High rate of fire wins when BTK rises by one, and is more forgiving in close quarters against a dodging target.

Drop, reloads, and being heard

  • Gravity: 11.25 for infantry weapons, 7.5 for bolt-action snipers, 8 for shotgun slugs. VS weapons have no drop except bolt-action snipers, launchers and slugs — but lower velocity on average. Lead moving targets; aim high at distance.
  • Reloads come in two lengths: short (magazine swapped, chamber not cycled) and long (empty magazine, chamber cycled). TRAC-5: 2.595 s short / 3.8 s long. Topping off is almost always the cheaper reload.
  • Fire detect range (unsuppressed) — how far your shots show you: 40–50 m for most infantry weapons, 60 m second-generation battle rifles, 100 m sniper rifles and rocket launchers, 150 m directive snipers and the Archer, 40 m TRAP-M1, 10 m Hunter crossbow, 5 m power knife. Compensator adds 35 m, flash suppressor adds 15 m, a suppressor removes it entirely.

02 Cone of fire

Bullets land randomly inside a cone, measured in degrees. The cone has a minimum, a current value and a maximum — and two stance rules that the whole ADAD rhythm is built on.

  • Bloom: every shot grows the current cone by the weapon's bloom per shot, up to its maximum. Higher-damage bullets usually bloom more. Hipfire bloom is usually double the ADS bloom (SMGs, the T7 MCG and most sniper rifles excepted).
  • Recovery: stop firing and the cone shrinks back to the stance minimum after a delay equal to the weapon's refire time, at 20°/s for most weapons. Worked example (TRAC-5, moving ADS): cone 0.3, ten shots bloom it 0.05 × 10 = 0.5 up to 0.8; refire is 60 ÷ 750 = 0.08 s, recovery takes (0.8 − 0.3) ÷ 20 = 0.025 s — full reset 0.105 s after the last shot.
  • Stance sets the floor: minimum and maximum depend on still / moving / crouching / jumping / falling, and on hipfire vs ADS. Sprint CoF is meaningless — you cannot fire while sprinting, and sprint recovery outlasts it. When you stop firing, the current cone drops to the stance minimum almost instantly.
  • Jumping kicks you out of ADS at once and gives most weapons a huge minimum cone mid-air. Exceptions: carbines and the Rocklet Rifle keep usable accuracy while jumping and flying — the basis of Light Assault aerial combat. Repeated jumps slow you down, stacking until you crawl. Falling a short distance (~5 m) counts as standing-moving, and you can ADS while falling.
CoF rule 1

The cone cannot shrink while you fire. Start firing from the hip and ADS mid-burst, and your cone stays as big as it was — only the bloom rate changes to the ADS value. Either don't ADS mid-burst, or stop firing for a moment to let it reset.

One exception: if the current cone exceeds the new stance's maximum, it snaps down to that maximum at once. The T7 MCG (fixed ADS cone) and the NSX Naginata (tiny still-ADS maximum) can exploit this.

CoF rule 2 — the ADAD rule

Stand still for the first shots, then move for free. Change stance while firing and, if your current cone is smaller than the new stance's minimum, it rises to match; if it is larger, it stays. So fire the opening shots standing still, and once bloom has pushed the cone past the moving minimum, start strafing — it costs no accuracy at all.

nstill = the shots it takes for the cone to bloom up to the moving minimum: the smallest n with still CoF + n × bloom ≥ moving min CoF. TRAC-5 (still 0.1, moving 0.3, bloom 0.05): 4 still shots, then move. Gauss Rifle (0.03, 0.3, 0.06): 5 still shots, then move.

ADS speed and scope-in

  • ADS movement speed is normally 0.5×. Some weapons allow 0.75× — 50% faster strafing while aimed, which makes their ADAD far more effective.   • Scope-in times: 150 ms for most weapons, 250–300 ms sniper rifles, 350 ms rocket launchers; an HS/NV scope adds 100 ms. Scope-out is 150 ms. These are animation times — the cone changes on its own schedule, which is why rule 1 bites when you fire too early.

03 Recoil

Vertical recoil is a mouse problem. Horizontal recoil is a burst-length problem. Confusing the two is the most common aiming error in the game.

What you can and cannot counter

  • Vertical recoil (kick up) is countered by pulling the mouse down the same amount, shot for shot.
  • Horizontal recoil (side-to-side rattle) is random per shot and cannot be countered by mouse movement — only corrected after the fact, and mitigated by shorter bursts.
  • Recoil angle: a random value between the weapon's angle min and max, rolled per shot, tilting the whole pattern — this is what makes a gun pull left or right. "Horizontal recoil bias" does not exist as a separate mechanic.
  • Recoil is measured in degrees and is absolute: a low-magnification scope does not have less recoil than a high-magnification one. The kick is identical; it just looks bigger zoomed in.

First-shot recoil multiplier

The FSRM multiplies the vertical recoil of the first shot of a burst, of every semi-auto shot, and of the first shot of a full-auto string. The TRAC-5's is 2.35×. This is the hidden tax on bursting and tap-firing: every burst pays it again. Counter it by aiming low in advance and letting the first kick carry the crosshair up onto the target.

It is also why the articles say to avoid single-shot and burst fire modes where possible — every shot in those modes takes the multiplier. Fixed-burst weapons are the exception.

Horizontal recoil tolerance — how far a burst can walk

The HRT is the angle around the first shot's crosshair position that bounds horizontal recoil. The rule: accumulated horizontal recoil can exceed half the tolerance once; the next kick is then forced the other way. So:

  • Nkicks = floor((HRT ÷ 2) ÷ minimum horizontal recoil) + 1
  • Pattern width = Nkicks × maximum horizontal recoil × 2

Dead — do not use

The HRT article shows cases where a Forward Grip could increase kicks and pattern width (Pulsar C the headline; Orion, Polaris, CARV-S, TAR and Equinox also listed). The article itself flags this as fixed in the 7 July 2016 patch. That list is history, not advice.

Recoil recovery and the pull-down quirk

  • After you stop firing, the crosshair returns after a true delay of refire time + recoil recovery delay. The recovery delay often equals the refire time, so the true delay is often 2× refire. Shotguns, battle rifles, semi-auto snipers and scouts start recovering earlier.
  • Recovery speed is the weapon's recoil decrease in degrees per second (TRAC-5: 18°/s).
  • The quirk: recovery only works properly if you don't move the crosshair to counteract vertical recoil. Pull down through a long burst and the crosshair barely moves when you release the trigger — the game was quietly cancelling your pull. On weapons you tap-fire, this decides your cadence: recentering time depends on vertical recoil, horizontal recoil, refire time, recovery delay and recovery rate. Fast tap firing wants high RoF, low recoil, low recovery delay, high recovery rate.
  • Some weapons scale recoil over a burst — the MGR-L1 Promise is the worked example, its vertical and horizontal recoil falling from maximum to minimum over the first 4–5 shots. Attachments don't change the scaling values.
  • The six recoil types rank from best — unvaried horizontal recoil, no angle, only the left/right direction random — to worst: varied horizontal recoil with varied angle, five random variables, typical of CQC weapons.

05 How and where to aim

Aiming is three steps — awareness, execution, confirmation — and the best players delete the third one.

The three steps, and skipping one

  1. Awareness — recognise the thing as an enemy.
  2. Execution — move the crosshair onto it.
  3. Confirmation — check the crosshair is on target, then fire.

Average human reaction time is ~250 ms and no training beats it. The only way past it is to predict where the enemy will be. Train slowly and deliberately; speed comes from muscle memory, and muscle memory needs one mouse sensitivity, kept constant.

Best players drop step 3. Twitch aiming fires on eye–brain–hand coordination with no confirmation, trading accuracy for speed. Drag shots are the extreme form, for weapons that live on a few accurate shots — shotguns, CQC sniping. Start twitch practice on a slow automatic; the guide suggests a 143-damage, 652-RPM weapon.

  • Track the crosshair, not the enemy. Eyes on the reticule, target in peripheral vision.
  • Pre-aiming: keep the crosshair where the enemy's head will appear — the edge of a doorway or corner, at head height — so they walk into your sights. Read the minimap and the friendly/enemy dots to predict the approach. Mid-fight, predict their strafe pattern and aim where they are about to move.
  • Pre-firing: start shooting on a guess before the enemy appears, then correct while firing. It backfires when the guess is wrong — you arrive at the real fight with a bloomed cone and a half-spent magazine.

Where to aim: centre mass, neck, or head

Aim pointWho / whenThe trade
Centre massBeginners and average players; compromised positionsMost bullets hit, TTK stays consistent. The head is roughly 4–5× smaller than the torso, so body shots are 4–5× easier to land; a headshot is worth 2× a body shot (2.5× against a Nanoweave target) — pure body shots are not a bad default.
NeckAbove-average players, most situationsSome bullets climb into the head from recoil and CoF; most still hit the body. The default of skilled play.

Skilled players use all three, by situation: a fair 1v1 inside optimal range, both firing at once → neck. A compromised position — sprinting, jumping, just finished a hipfire kill, cone in a bad state → centre mass. An unaware enemy with a clear head shot → ADS and go for the head. Range bends the same way: close → head, further → neck, outside effective range → centre mass.

06 Hipfire vs ADS — the grey area

Every weapon's effective range splits into a hipfire part and an ADS part. Very close is hipfire, far is ADS — and between them sits the decision that defines mid-level play.

Attachments move the boundary, not the total: a Laser Sight extends hipfire range (not total range); a Forward Grip extends total range (not hipfire range); a Compensator extends total range but shrinks hipfire range; a large magazine extends hipfire range because misses are less punishing.

Hipfire is a hose

Easier to land at least some damage, and your faster movement dodges more of theirs — but it spends more ammo and gives a longer TTK. Hipfire is a bet on the enemy having bad accuracy: safe against most players, fatal against a good shot.

ADS is a drill

The tightest stream, the fastest possible TTK, the least ammo — if you actually hit. ADS is a bet on your accuracy. Its costs: scope-in time, narrower field of view, amplified screen shake and flinch, the gun and scope blocking vision, slower movement — and if you start firing before the ADS finishes, you keep the hipfire cone penalties (rule 1, §02). Rule: ADS only for the duration of the engagement.

The seven factors

Inside the grey area, the real question is "is ADSing necessary?" Weigh:

  1. Your accuracy vs the enemy's.
  2. Target status — how much damage the kill still needs. Heavies need more; a wounded or shield-flashing enemy needs less.
  3. Target position and cover — a partly exposed target wants ADS.
  4. Target movement — a fast-moving target wants hipfire.
  5. Ammo left.
  6. Weapon stats — high RoF/DPS and good hipfire CoF favour hipfire; 0.75× ADS speed favours ADS. A good hipfire SMG with Advanced Laser Sight adds a third option: hipfiring for headshots.
  7. Space to manoeuvre — no space argues against hipfire; space itself is neutral.

Simple answers. ADS when ammo is short, when you must kill fastest or die, when the enemy is still or slow, when the enemy is partly behind cover. Hipfire when you are in danger and cannot afford to slow down, or when you can trade extra ammo and time for movement and less damage taken.

Halospud's shorter version: always ADS at range; always ADS against a head-glitching enemy; ADS when low on health and needing a fast headshot kill; ADS against a stationary enemy; always hipfire in extreme close quarters. Hipfire more with carbines and SMGs.

07 Bursting

Bursts keep the cone tight, keep you short of the horizontal-recoil tolerance limit, and open gaps to correct your aim. Every gap also re-charges the first-shot multiplier — so keep gaps as short as possible to protect DPS.

Burst length is not a number; it depends on:

  • Initial CoF — the stance you start from.
  • Bloom per shot — how fast the cone grows.
  • Distance to target — the same cone covers less of a far target.
  • Whether the crosshair is still on target. If you lost it, stop firing and let the cone reset while you re-acquire. Firing past the target blooms the gun and wastes ammo — worst on high-RoF guns and low-magazine guns against shielded Heavies.
  • Risk. Target about to reach cover, about to kill you, a third party arriving — sometimes the right call is to gamble and keep firing.

The practical rule: keep firing until you see your bullets missing. With experience you develop an "inner clock" for each situation — that clock, not a shot count, is the skill.

Dead — do not use

The Weapon Mechanics guide contains an angular-size burst-length calculation. The guide itself marks the angular-size research 💀 DEAD — "proven to be wrong. Do not use it" (Dorf's projectile-radius finding, 0.03 m, corrected it). Its burst-length numbers and effective-range tables do not carry over. Burst by the factors above.

Semi-autos

Tap-fire: aim at the head, fire, wait for the crosshair to fully return, fire again — the double-tap headshot on semi-auto snipers is the template. Use the queue trick (§04) so the weapon, not your finger, sets the pace. Spamming is fine at close range with pistols and shotguns, where recentering matters less than volume.

08 Movement & the ADAD rhythm

A stationary target is very easy to kill. Movement is armour — but only movement timed to the cone rules is free.

The rhythm

ADAD — alternating strafe keys — makes the enemy miss, but the timing is the whole trick: too long between switches and the enemy re-acquires you; too short and you barely move, so they hit you by accident while aiming at a rough area. Halospud's advice is qualitative only: most players leave too long between switches, so if in doubt, alternate a little quicker.

The numeric layer comes from CoF rule 2 (§02): fire the first shots standing still, then strafe once the cone has bloomed past the moving minimum — from that shot on, movement costs no accuracy. 0.75× ADS weapons make the strafe itself 50% faster and the whole rhythm much more effective. Outside that opening, moving while shooting is nearly always worth it: the small cone improvement from standing still is worth less than the misses your movement forces.

Where the numbers live

There is no numeric ADAD cadence in the Halospud articles — anyone quoting one from them is inventing it. The counts come from the CoF derivation above, verified live in VR Training in 2026, and the per-weapon still-shot counts are on the ADAD calculator.

Reloading and range discipline

  • Reload in cover. Check the minimap and look around while you do it.
  • Caught mid-reload: run, or draw the sidearm — equipping it is always faster than finishing a reload.
  • Don't reload after every kill ("reloaditus"). If the magazine still holds enough for one more enemy, stay ready; check you are safe first.
  • Get inside your weapon's effective range before opening fire. The articles' rough pointers: shotguns 5 m, SMGs 20 m, carbines 40 m, assault rifles 60 m, LMGs 80 m, semi-auto snipers 100 m, bolt-actions 150–300 m by model. Being able to see a target does not mean you should shoot it.

09 The 1v1 duel

An engagement starts when either side rounds a corner into line of sight. How you handle the peek matters as much as how you aim.

  • Crosshair placement: aim at the edge of the corner where the enemy's head will appear, at head height, before they arrive; predict the approach from the map and the dots. This matters most while learning a low sensitivity, because it removes the big mouse swings needed to acquire targets.
  • Minimise line of sight: round a corner only far enough to see one enemy. Peeking too far against several is a big disadvantage — take them one at a time and chain the kills. Staying close to cover means you can duck back the moment you start losing. Avoid 1v2s while learning. Tight peeking limits your own dodging, so headshots matter more there — and bring the highest-DPS CQC weapon you can.
  • Peek in hipfire, ADS after. ADSing before the corner slows your strafe and throws away your peek advantage. Peek hipfiring, ADS the instant you see the opponent — unless the weapon hipfires well or the enemy is right in front of you. And never start firing before the ADS finishes: you keep the hipfire cone penalties even once aimed.
  • Stalemates: when both sides pre-aim a doorway, pushing in wears you down a little with every peek. Flank instead — find a less defended entrance.
Peek-first advantage

The aggressor gets your ping + server delay + the opponent's ping before the opponent's client even shows you peeking — time for a shot or two, free. Slow-peeking (ADS-strafing around a corner) negates it entirely. Worse, your shoulder, feet or weapon can be visible around the corner before you can see them. Move fast: strafe out hipfiring, ADS on sight. It also works defensively — peek and duck back repeatedly until someone is out there.

Partial cover and head-glitching

Your viewpoint is at your head/neck and your bullets come from there, not from the gun — so behind the right cover you can see much more of the enemy than they see of you. The rule: the player closest to the apex of the cover wins. The classic spots are the tops of stairs. The price is built in: if they do hit you, it is almost certainly a headshot — take any damage in a head-glitch spot and back away at once. Watch for hitboxes extending past the visible edge: aiming just above railings and barriers often hits the railing; stairs, crates and terrain suffer less.

Fight selection — read the threat level first

Before you commit, weigh: are you already under fire? Your cover and escape route? Who has the initiative — they fired first unseen is much worse; you see them and they don't know is much better; you caught them reloading is much better. Relative skill, if known. Whether the enemy is inside their weapon's effective range. Whether other enemies are nearby.

Too high → disengage and restart on better terms. If you must fight at high threat, the higher it is, the more you are pushed to ADS and go for the fastest possible kill.

Evasive movement (Halospud, Part 4)

Evasive movement works best in close quarters, and geometry is why: at 90° horizontal field of view, an enemy 5 m away must run 10 m to cross your screen; at 18 m away, 36 m. Far targets cross slowly and are easy to track — but they are also smaller, so at long range the small recoil kicks matter more. Use smooth mouse movement when aiming and fast, twitchy movement when dodging; twitchy movement also breaks up your character's animations, so your feet slide and give no readable direction of travel. Low frame rate makes twitch aim inconsistent.

Warping is mostly not latency

Rapid erratic movement makes character animations fail to match what you are doing — feet sliding, landing animations playing while you keep running and change direction. Genuine packet-loss warping exists, but it is the rarer case. What looks like a cheater's movement is usually this.

Sprint strafing

Sprint one way, spin 180° and keep sprinting — no speed is lost, because the game has no movement acceleration. Repeat with short gaps so the enemy never settles their aim; it works best perpendicular to the opponent. It buys time, not distance: use it while reloading, healing, or caught unprepared — then stop and aim the moment the enemy stops firing (a reload or weapon swap), and aim for the head, since you are likely low on health.

Zig-zagging

The same idea with shallower turns (~135°), so you still make progress in a direction at the cost of less dodging. Good against semi-auto and bolt-action weapons and for crossing sniper-covered ground. Automatics will still land shots — just fewer.

The Therum Shuffle

Zig-zagging with 80–100° turns, constant small mouse twitches to slide the feet, and jumps — landing bends the knees while you keep running, which makes the model slide. As a Heavy, jump to tap overshield and take a medkit in the air (no movement penalty there); never use overshield to shuffle on the ground, it halves your speed. Don't jump repeatedly — it applies a speed penalty. Advanced, and most players are better off sprint strafing. You cannot see your own animations, so practise with someone watching on the Test Server, Jaeger or in VR.

Sprint peeking

Instead of strafing out of a corner, sprint out, turn ~90° to aim, and strafe back into the corner as you turn. It maximises peek-first advantage, and you clear the corner further than a strafing peek, so a pre-aimed enemy has to re-acquire. Adding a crouch after the strafe throws a head-level pre-aim off again. Downsides: you expose yourself to more enemies, and you need fast, reliable aim or the advantage is wasted. It works better with higher ping and slow server tick — large prime-time fights.

Repeeking and doubling back

When you break off, turn and peek again the instant you reach cover. Repeeking re-takes peek-first advantage and, more importantly, surprises people — they are often sprinting, falling back, or slow to react. It is devastating against compulsive reloaders. It does not work against an enemy holding a position or chokepoint — they are still ADSing and ready. Doubling back is a repeek with a delay, around small buildings or crates: keep sprinting a little past cover (they hear the footsteps), then turn and repeek from deeper in cover. The enemy thinks you went the other way — they either chase and meet you with their weapon down, or turn to cut you off on the far side and take shots in the back.

Jump pads and human cover

You keep some control in the air off a jump pad: hold jump for more height, crouch for downward speed, directional keys plus mouse for momentum. Touching a pad gives a few seconds of fall-damage immunity. Beat the "turkey shoot" — enemies farming the top of a vertical pad — by holding crouch at the top and walking straight out instead of flying up. On long pads into camped elevated platforms, drop a little short and use the platform as cover. And enemies themselves are cover: peek so only one enemy has line of sight while the other sees only his teammate's back — he may even shoot his teammate. In very dense rooms enemies block each other's lines of fire; that crowding is what knife-primary play exploits, and it works for any class with good movement, awareness and fast aim. High risk, high reward.

10 Awareness & the net

In the ideal fight you never play on reaction — you force the enemy to. Awareness is what makes that possible, and the netcode decides what "fair" even means.

  • Awareness is a stream: weapon sounds identify the class; faction voices give away faction and status; one sniper tracer in peripheral vision is enough to locate a camper. Keep a mental list of enemies who revealed themselves until you kill them or see the corpse. One eye on the minimap, both ears open.
  • You fight in the past. Internet delay means you see what the enemy was doing ~200 ms ago, and they see the same of you. Hit detection is client-side: if they shot your model on their screen, you take damage — which is why you can die after reaching cover. Turn it around: storming a room, you see the defenders before they see you. Start dodging before the enemy fires, get to cover the moment you take fire, and never stop to look for the shooter when under fire — the minimap hit direction is enough to pick a direction to run. The Counter-Intelligence implant helps by spotting anyone who shoots you with an unsuppressed weapon.
  • Your camera is in your chest. Your first-person camera sits in the chest area of your model, so your head is higher than where you look from — peeking over cover, your head can stick out above what you see. Field of view is narrow (85–106° horizontal at the common 55–74° vertical settings), so approaching from the side, even slightly to the front, is safe unless they turn. And you cannot tell whether an enemy is ADSing: the third-person ADS animation lags seconds behind.
  • Tracers are not damage. The enemy's tracers are rendered by your client and can appear to hit you while you take no damage — and vice versa. Judge by health, not by tracers.
  • Footsteps: crouch-walking is audible at ~20 m, sprinting and landing at ~40 m in ideal conditions — rarely audible in a real fight, so don't rely on it.

Player model quirks worth knowing

  • Looking down bends the model forward and hides the head from behind — useful when running from a sniper, combined with erratic movement.
  • Sprinting with a pistol puts your arms partly over your head.
  • Moving while crouched behind cover unbends the model and your head can poke out.
  • Crouching while jumping or on a jet pack does nothing until you land.
  • Your lowest profile: crouching, still, looking down.

11 Per-class essentials

The mechanics above, bent by each class. Loadout specifics in the class guides date from 2016–2017; the roles and the numbers that define them are the durable part.

Light Assault flanking and harassing

A mobile flanker: the jet pack bypasses defences, scales obstacles and attacks from unexpected angles. There is no Heavy staying power — mobility is the defence. LAs often fight alone, away from medics, and rely on stationary spawns. Carbines are the primary and uniquely keep walking hipfire accuracy while jumping and flying — the basis of LA aerial combat. SMGs are defensive (hipfire and mobility, short range); shotguns are for killing vulnerable enemies at extremely short range.

  • Skirmisher — the versatile default jet pack (fuel/recharge +8/11/14/17/20% by rank).
  • Drifter — maximises horizontal glide (15 s air at rank 1, +4/7/11/15 s, 6 s recharge) for travelling between bases, not for fighting inside buildings.
  • Icarus — pure vertical thrust, fast recharge, poor air control; it overshoots ledges, so release jump early. The article's tests put the highest survivable fall with Icarus and no Safe Fall at ~75 m.
  • Ambusher — fires you forward (cooldown 8 s down to 6 s) and stays useful in combat; jump from cover to cover. Safe Fall is called nearly mandatory.
  • Rocklet Rifle (tool) — moderate damage to vehicles and hovering aircraft, accurate while flying. C4 is the answer to everything at close range.
  • The "Hanger" trick: float above the enemy where there is nowhere else to hide — high ceiling, nearby combat masking the sound of the jets.

Combat Medic assault rifles, sustain, revives

Support that fights: assault rifles (called the best automatics in the game), self-heal, revives — including mass revives from a distance with revive grenades — and C4. Strongest in drawn fights with room to move. You must be able to heal yourself: Nano-Regen Device, medkits, or the Regeneration implant (10 s delay after health damage, then ~25 s to full at tier 1, ~15 s at tier 4–5 — the article's numbers).

  • Revive discipline: don't overextend for one person; check the killer is no longer a threat; never revive someone who died in a guarded position. Step out, lock on, jump back into cover; stay mobile while reviving; don't walk around with the applicator in hand.
  • Revive priority: in safety, ending death timers first (flashing skull), MAXes included, then other medics. Compromised → medics first. Overrun → Heavies first, then medics. Don't waste revive grenades on one person.
  • Ability tricks: Nano-Regen can tank one or two extra bullets if activated just as damage starts; it stacks with the applicator to heal ~60% faster, and passively heals nearby allies while you keep aiming. The Shield Recharging Field directs allied flow and doubles as bait. The applicator itself can bait: start a revive on an exposed corpse, don't finish it, and catch the enemy who rushes you.

Infiltrator cloak is concealment, not stealth

Cloaking makes you harder to see, not invisible. There are five visibility stages by leg state; slower means less visible, and crouching still is deep cloak — almost invisible. Cloak and decloak each make a faction-specific sound. While cloaked you can capture points, reload and resupply, and you are detected by motion sensors; you cannot fire or use tools, regenerate health or shields, or be spotted — cloaking removes the spotted condition. Cloak can work against you: you cannot shoot first while cloaked, and the tiny decloak delay can cost the opening shot. Think of cloak as concealment — they know you exist but not where — not stealth; only Stalker cloak gives enough time to build the doubt true stealth needs.

  • Shield shimmer: taking damage while cloaked makes your shields shimmer and clearly visible; shields flash again when they break. The Chameleon Module removes the shimmer on hit (not on shield break).
  • Hunter cloak — 12 s, drains while cloaked. Nano-Armor — +100 shields, 25–35% small-arms resistance while cloaked, 6.5–8.5 s; the aggressive option. Stalker — energy drains while moving cloaked, recharges while still cloaked or uncloaked, potentially indefinite, but no primary weapon.
  • Darklight flashlight reveals cloaked infiltrators at ~12 m — and its beam goes through walls.
  • Roles. Stalker: scouting, sabotage, denying terminals and turrets. Sniper: semi-auto takes two headshots, bolt-action one; counter snipers by staying indoors, moving constantly, or hunting them — tracers and sound give them away. CQC SMG: Advanced Laser Sight called mandatory, ADS only when you can do so freely, and the whole game is catching people unaware — the article's target is 70% of encounters against heavily distracted enemies. Chain headshots against Heavies. If you are engaged on, do not cloak and sit still — run to cover, medkit, re-engage.
  • Hacking covers terminals, Phalanx turrets and Spitfires. Tools: Recon Detection Device, Motion Spotter, and the EMP grenade, which drains shields.

Heavy Assault the aim class

The overshield is a shield layer that makes you much harder to kill but slows you 25% (multiplicative). Personal shields don't regenerate while it is active; if energy is fully drained there is a 3 s delay before regeneration starts. An overshielded Heavy can survive normally lethal things: a direct Decimator hit, a sniper headshot (not always), a power-knife body hit, a point-blank pump-action body shot.

Engineer deployables, repairs, ammo

  • MANA anti-personnel turret: a 143-damage machine gun (143 @ 10 m – 125 @ 65 m, 550 RPM) with no recoil and a heat mechanic instead of ammo, behind a front shield invulnerable to all damage — but the engineer's head and the gun base stay exposed.
  • MANA anti-vehicle turret: laser-guided missiles, 700 direct damage, 10–14 shots per minute — comparable to a starting rocket launcher.
  • Spitfire auto-turret: engages hostile infantry within 50 m on its own; warns you by sound and by shooting; can be destroyed by small arms or disabled by EMP; ignores cloaked infiltrators; infiltrators can hack it.
  • Repair tool: 125 HP/s unupgraded, 10 s before overheating; max rank repairs 1000 HP in 6 s. It also safely deconstructs enemy tank mines and C4. Rumble-seat repairs run at 60%, one engineer at a time.
  • Ammo packs: resupply allies and yourself with ammunition, rockets, UBGL grenades and infiltrator radar tools. Enemy packs work too. Packs slide down slopes and can be destroyed by enemy fire or EMP.
  • Sticky grenade: 1250 damage @ 2.5 m down to 10 @ 7 m; lethal to standard infantry within 3.4 m (~50% further than a normal frag). The article notes a bug where it sometimes deals double damage.
  • Nano-repair grenade: 50 HP over 10 s to all repairable allied units — less than a third of a maxed repair tool; one active at a time.
  • NS-AM7 Archer: bolt-action, great hipfire accuracy, heavy damage to MAXes, minor damage to vehicles, destroys spawn beacons in one shot.
  • Engineers' shields start recharging in 4 s instead of 6 s. Two tank mines usually destroy most vehicles and kill MAXes even with Ordnance Armor.

MAX a support-dependent battering ram

2000 HP, no shields. MAX weapons are balanced around being used as a pair — the default mixed loadout (one anti-infantry arm, one anti-air or anti-vehicle) is effective at nothing; budget 650–1000 certs for a second matching weapon. TR/VS anti-infantry MAX guns work to 20–40 m; NC ones are shotguns, devastating in CQC and weak at range.

  • Resistances: 80% vs small arms (100 damage becomes 20), 90% vs melee, 100% vs flak — but −65% vs C4 and −85% vs tank mines, −25% vs heavy anti-vehicle weapons and the Archer. One C4 brick barely kills a full-health MAX within 2.5 m. Headshots still deal bonus damage.
  • Armour slots: Kinetic Armor adds 20% bullet resistance (80% → 84% behind the scenes); Ordnance Armor adds explosive resistances by rank; Nanite Auto Repair heals 0.5–1.5% after an 8 s delay. Emergency Repair restores 20% over 12 s on a 45–60 s cooldown.
  • Faction abilities: TR Anchor (immobile, big firepower increase); VS Zealot Overdrive (faster, up to +10% damage close, but +20% damage taken, 16–25 s, 60 s recharge); NC Aegis shield (front cover, can reload behind it, grenades don't stick, rockets hitting it deal no splash — but enemies can walk through it point-blank).
  • MAXes are weak alone: they need Engineer attention, cannot drive, and are big, slow, loud targets with faction-specific footsteps. Stick with the team; below half health, hide and call for an Engineer.

Fighting a MAX

A straight 1v1 is next to impossible. Gun it down with concentrated small-arms fire; rocket it from behind cover as a Heavy; lure it onto C4 or tank mines; approach unseen and put explosives under its feet; or kill its support — medics and engineers — first and whittle it down. Stay far away when possible: MAXes have no zoom and no long-range anti-infantry weaponry.

12 What still holds — and what doesn't

This guide is distilled from 2016 articles. The archive's own currency labels, carried through honestly:

Timeless — game constants

The CoF system (min/current/max, bloom per shot, recovery at 20°/s after a refire-time delay, and the two stance-change rules), the recoil structure, the damage model, movement speeds, sprint recovery, ADS slowdown, scope-in times, fire detect ranges and equip times are mechanics of the engine, not balance numbers. The archive labels Weapon Mechanics method timeless, data dated, and the CoF stance rules plus the ideal-ADAD rhythm are verified in live VR Training (2026-11-09). The Halospud articles are labelled principles, qualitative.

Dated — verify against current data

Every weapon-stat example here is from 2016 — the TRAC-5 and Gauss Rifle worked examples, the 0.75× ADS list, the HRT table. They illustrate the method; current values live in the Sage guides and the compare page. Nanoweave values have changed since 2016 — the leg-shot maths with its 0.8 factor (1 × 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72×) and the "up to 25% effective HP" figure are period values, not current ones, so this guide states the base multipliers (×2 head, ×0.9 legs) and leaves attachment-modified values to current Census data. Class-guide loadouts, cert costs and ranks are 2016–2017 (with a 2019 Ambusher update note): mechanics durable, specifics dated. The Gunplay Guide and class guides carry no dedicated currency label in the archive index — read them as 2016 mechanics writing: principles durable, weapon examples dated.

Dead — excluded from this guide

Angular size — marked 💀 DEAD by the Weapon Mechanics guide itself ("proven to be wrong. Do not use it."), which kills its burst-length calculation and effective-range tables; they are not reproduced here. Forward Grip making weapons worse — fixed in the 7 July 2016 patch, per the HRT article itself. Two smaller gaps, stated plainly: the articles' "CoF size increase speed is 50 degrees per second" line is unexplained and unclear next to per-shot bloom, so nothing here leans on it; and recoil recovery acceleration is explicitly unknown (a Wrel quote in the source: "We don't know much about this parameter"). The burst-weapon melee/grenade trick and the pump/bolt melee-during-chamber trick are described in the sources as bugs that may be fixed — possibly not working in 2026, so they are left out rather than taught.

One more honest gap: the beginner pages the Gunplay Guide links to ("how to kill / survive / capture a base") are lost — no snapshot exists in the archive. And the video links throughout the original articles are mostly dead or unverifiable; nothing in this guide depends on them.
