Evasive Movement Concepts
Mouse Movements
When aiming in Planetside it’s generally a good idea to make fast but smooth mouse movements.  A lower frame rate affects your mouse sensitivity, particularly for faster, twitchier movements.  As frame rate is dynamic and constantly changing, this can mean that Twitch aim shots can yield inconsistent results.  This effect reduces with higher FPS but smooth mouse movements are generally the way to go.


However, when dodging fire, rapid, twitchy mouse movements are excellent because they create more erratic movement and they also mess up the character animations more, making your feet slide around without giving any real indication of the direction of travel.


You’ll notice when watching videos of the best “movement meta” players such as D3S and Therum, that their mouse movements are very twitchy and constant.  They don’t stop moving and changing direction.  Here’s an example of D3S in a situation that seems impossible, using maximum movement meta to survive and keep killing.




How It Works
Works Best in CQC
Firstly it works a lot better in close quarters.  This is simply down to field of vision.  Basically the further out from you a target is, the more distance they have to cover in order to cross your screen.  


  



The diagram above illustrates the point for a character with a horizontal FOV of 90 degrees.  At 5m out an enemy has to run 10m to cross the player’s monitor.  At 18m out the same player has to run 36m.  This takes much longer, resulting in slower movement across the screen which is much easier to track.


There is a trade-off in that distant targets are much smaller than close ones which does mean that you have to be more precise in order to hit them (you have a smaller area of the screen to aim at.)  This becomes more significant at longer ranges where the small kicks of recoil are enough to make a difference but it is still much harder to accurately track in close quarters because the target is going faster and you have to make bigger and faster mouse movements to accommodate for that.




Warping
There are two different effects that people see in game that they class as warping.  The first one is genuine packet loss / high latency which can certainly create warped movements that are very hard to track.  The second and much more common one is misleading character animations.


If you run forwards in Planetside your characters legs move in such a way that makes it look like they are running.  In simple situations this works fine.  However when you perform rapid, erratic movements you can make it so that these animations don’t actually match what you’re doing and this can create a warping-like effect..


The simplest way to see this is by running in zig zags and strafing at the same time and you’ll notice that your character’s feet are not working the way they should but are actually sliding along the ground a bit because the character animations can’t properly show what you’re doing.


Another one is when you jump and land, the character animation puts both feet down as if to absorb the impact of landing, but you can still keep running and change direction through this which makes your character just slide along with both feet flat to the ground.


This isn’t really latency or packet loss related warping, it’s just the fact that you can move in game in ways that it’s nearly impossible to make a character model accurately reflect.  When you combine one of the longer animations with a change of sprint direction this especially can look like warping.


This slowed down footage demonstrates the point quite well.




Using Enemies as Cover
The simplest example of this is if you peek a corner and there are two enemies very close together with a line of sight on you.  Alternatively you could peek a little further out (see sprint peeking below) so that only one of them has a line of sight on you and the other can only see his teammate's back.  If you’re lucky, he might even shoot his team mate in the back trying to get you.


At a more advanced level you take the somewhat high risk strategy of using excessive enemy population density to your advantage.  There comes a point where there are so many enemies inside a room that they are actually hindering each other.  They move constantly, crossing and blocking each others’ lines of sight and it’s this effect that the knife primary playstyle basicly exploits.


However, you can exploit is as any infantry class with good movement, awareness and fast aim.  Here’s the same clip of D3S from earlier, doing just that.


Note how many medics there are with med tools out running across their teammates line of fire.  This is their safe space, filled with their teammates.  They just aren’t expecting an enemy to be there and they have very poor awareness.


However be clear that D3S still uses the simple concept of minimising line of sight by going straight for the alcove under the stairs and also note how he quickly reprioritises and switches targets when one of them starts firing at him.


This aggressive use of enemies as cover is high risk and difficult to master.  It’s also difficult to learn when it’s a viable option but it’s a lot of fun and highly rewarding in terms of lots of kills in not much time.




Movement Tricks
Sprint Strafing
Regular Sprint Strafe Dodging
Sprint strafing is a way of mimicking ADAD movement but using full speed sprinting.  It is one of the most effective forms of dodging enemy fire available and doesn’t depend on creating much of a warping effect.  The way it works is simply down to movement speed and changes of direction in a game that has no movement acceleration, so you can spin around 180 degrees without losing any speed.


It’s called sprint strafing because the changes of direction are done to mirror ADAD movement but at a much faster speed.


Basically you sprint in a direction and then continue to sprint but very quickly turn 180 degrees with no loss of speed.  As with ADAD movement, the changes of direction should be repeated and done with quite short gaps in between so that your opponent doesn’t have enough time to get their aim on point in between turns.


Sprint strafing is most effective when done at an angle perpendicular to your opponent.  This maximises how fast you move across your opponents screen.


  

Sprint strafing is a valuable survival technique that buys you time.  When you get caught reloading, taking a medkit or are otherwise unprepared to engage it can be used to gain precious seconds.  In this clip VIRT kills a couple of enemies and then gets caught by a third while reloading.  He quickly sprint strafes and then snaps to target as soon as the reload is complete, securing the kill.  Without the sprint strafe he would almost certainly have died.


In this clip VIRT gets shot at by an enemy that he doesn’t expect and doesn’t immediately know where he is.  He immediately starts sprinting around until the attacker backs off to reload.  VIRT uses this opportunity to place his crosshairs where the enemy was and when he repeeks, the kill is quite straightforward.  He then gets caught reloading by a second enemy and zigzags to reduce the number of hits taken before he can shoot.


There comes a point when you have to decide to stop sprint strafing and the right time to do this varies with the situation.  If you were doing it to buy time to complete a reload you might decide to stop and aim as soon as the reload completes.  However, if you can hear that your opponent is using a Carbine or Assault Rifle (weapons with smaller magazines) you might wait a little longer until he stops firing.  This would indicate that either a reload or switch to secondary is taking place, giving you a short opportunity to aim without being shot.


As a general rule, if the other guy stops firing at all, that’s your opportunity to try and take him.  In such situations you should always try to aim for the head, as you’re likely a little low on health and shields and need to make up for that with a rapid TTK.




Zig-Zagging
The disadvantage of sprint strafing is that you don’t actually get anywhere.  It’s very effective dodging but you aren’t escaping, just buying time.  The alternative that allows you to still make some progress, is zig-zagging.


In effect this is exactly the same thing but you change the 180 degree turns for a shallower angle (135 for instance)  this means that you can still move in a direction while dodging.


The downside is that the dodging is less effective because your movement speed across your opponents monitor is reduced due to the angle you’re running at.


This well against semi-auto and bolt action weapons and can be used to cross ground that’s covered by enemy snipers if you absolutely have to.  Automatic weapons will generally still land shots on you, although a lot fewer than if you were running in a straight line.




Therum Shuffle
The Therum shuffle is a technique named after the player that made it famous.  It’s very similar to sprint strafing or zig-zagging but with embellishments.  This should definitely be considered an advanced technique.  Most players are probably better off just sprint strafing.


A lot of people confuse shuffling and sprint strafing but the major difference between them is that shuffling messes up your character animations more, making it harder to read where you’re going.


Start off with zig-zagging, doing repeated 80-100 degree turns across the line you want to travel in.  However, don’t run in straight lines, keep twitching the mouse a bit to make your feet slide around in the running animation.  


Jumping is a key part of the shuffle because when you land your character model bends its knees to soak up the inertia, but your character can keep running which creates even more weird sliding.


As a Heavy Assault, the jumps also give you an opportunity to tap your overshield on and take a medkit.  It’s better to do this in the air because then it results in no less of movement speed.  Be careful not to hit jump too many times in quick succession though, as this will apply a movement speed penalty.


The overshield should not be used to shuffle while on the ground because it halves your movement speed.


This is what shuffling looks like with a little packet loss.  The warping and misleading animations become more extreme and the player becomes extremely difficult to shoot.  If you have packet loss like this then you should not be playing Planetside but perhaps a slightly higher ping playing at a time when the servers are running slow and you can get a much reduced level of this effect.


As you can’t see your own character animations, mastering the art of shuffling takes two people.  You really have to practice it with someone watching you so you can establish what mouse and keyboard techniques work best and how to time them for the best effect.  You can do this on the Test Server, Jaeger or simply in VR Training.




Sprint Peeking
In the 2nd guide of this series we discussed how to peek by strafing out from a corner with disciplined crosshairs placement and only ADSing when you’re out from the corner so that you maximise movement speed.  Sprint peeking is an alternative and more advanced method.


Instead of strafing out from the corner you sprint out and then make a roughly 90 degree turn to aim at your target.  At the same time as you turn to aim, strafe with the turn, so you’re strafing back into the corner.


The advantage of this is that by sprinting out you can maximise peek first advantage.  You’re also not limited to peeking by the edge of the corner; you can sprint a little further out so that if your opponent was pre-aiming the corner, you’re out of their crosshairs again and they have some work to do to catch up.


Strafing back into the corner is also significant.  Going from sprinting forwards to strafing the opposite direction can create a small warping effect on your opponent’s client that makes you a little harder to initially aim at.


Also the player model animations in Planetside are not great and can be very misleading, especially with rapid mouse movements and changes of direction.  The way that Planetside displays a quick flick to your target while also strafing back from your sprint direction can be very misleading for the other guy, throwing his aim.


Adding a crouch just after the strafe can throw a pre-aiming player off again, as he was probably aiming at head level.


The downsides of this are that you need to peek further out from the corner which risks exposing you to multiple enemies.  You also need to aim very quickly and accurately or the peek advantage that you gain is not fully exploited or can even be completely lost.  This is an advanced technique and I would consider fast, reliable aim to be a prerequisite for it.


In theory this will work a little better when you have a higher ping and when the server tick rate is slow (large fights at prime time.)  These two things will give you some more ms of advantage and can exaggerate the warping effect a little more.




Repeeking
Regular Repeeks
Repeeking is a very simple concept but perhaps counter-intuitive.  The idea is that when backing out of an engagement, just as you reach cover, you immediately turn and peek again.


In this clip MrGuanabana is lining up a shot on an enemy when he gets surprised from a different angle by another enemy.  He quickly disengages and runs to cover to break line of sight.  He then immediately repeeks and takes the kill.


Repeeking means you take the peek first advantage for yourself again but the real benefit is just how much it surprises people.  You can very often catch them sprinting, falling back or just very slow to react as they’re not expecting it.


One scenario where MrGuanabana uses this a lot is when he peeks two enemies.  So he’ll kill one, break line of sight and then immediately repeek the second as in this clip.  He surprises the second enemy, catching him in the middle of a grenade throw and takes no further damage.  Repeeking works especially well against compulsive reloaders as catching someone reloading in this way is basically a free kill.  


Repeeking is not effective against an enemy who is holding a position, a chokepoint for example.  When you repeek they will often still be ADSing and ready to fire.  It works much better away from entrenched positions.




Doubling Back
A double back is basically exactly the same as a repeek but with more delay.  It works specifically around very small buildings or crates where you could run around the left or you could run around the right.


When you sprint back into cover instead of immediately repeeking, keep sprinting a little further (hopefully they can subconsciously hear the footsteps) and turn around to repeek when you’re a bit deeper into cover.  The idea of this is that your opponent will think that you’ve gone the other way due to the delay and hearing you continue to sprint so he has two options: He can sprint to chase you (in which case you will catch him with his weapon down) or he will turn around and go the other way to catch you on the other side where he expects you to have gone.  In this case you can shoot him in the back.


Doubling back is a way of playing mind games with your opponent by trying to mislead them so that they do something that gives you an advantage (going the wrong way or sprinting just before you peek.)




Jump Pad Control
When using jump pads you still have some control over your movement speed and direction in the air.  Holding the jump key will add vertical movement speed, holding the crouch key will add downward movement speed.  The forward and backward keys also work and you can use them with the mosue to add momentum in any direction that you want..


Sometimes you can use this to your advantage.


Note: Using a jump pad gives you fall damage immunity for a short period, so simply touching a jump pad allows you to jump from any realistic height without taking any fall damage but only for a few seconds.




Beating the Turkey-Shoot
The simplest way to use this is to avoid the “turkey shoot” at the top of jump pads.  This where people fly up in the air above a vertical jump pad and are easily killed before they land.


By holding your crouch key at the top of the jump pad, you can avoid flying up into the air and walk straight out onto the ground.  This means you can aim faster and don’t become such an easy target.


An example of this can be seen here.




Dropping Short
The long distance jump pads usually lead to an elevated platform.  These platforms are often camped and the elevated position means there are lots of angles / lines of sight on you.  However by dropping a little short you can often use these platforms as cover.


To do this you can hold the control key or aim backwards and down while holding your forward key.  By doing this in a slightly more measured way you can do the same thing when jumping up to Bio Labs.  In this case simply holding the keys for the whole jump will mean you land below the pads in open space.  Carefully judging how much downward motion you need though, can result in this.


In some bases there are even nice vantage points that you can get to this way.




TLDR
* Dodging fire works best in CQC.
* When moving evasively, use twitchier mouse movements, sprinting and rapid changes of direction.
* Add strafing and jumps to make your character animations misleading, which is often perceived as warping.
* You can use enemies to block each others’ lines of sight.
